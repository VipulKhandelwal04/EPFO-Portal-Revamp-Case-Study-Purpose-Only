# International analogues per candidate: how other countries solved it, what broke, what India can lift

**Date: 2026-08-29.** For each of the 18 candidates on `problem-discovery-ledger.md` (ordered by the 2026-08-29 buildability re-rank): the named foreign mechanism that addressed the same failure, how it works, its honest pitfalls, and the smallest transplantable slice — (a) what the Indian state could copy cheaply, (b) what our citizen-side tool (no govt API, no govt adoption) can borrow. Researched by 4 parallel agents with per-claim source verification; failed leads are noted, not hidden. Companion files: `candidate-complaint-scan-2026-08-29.md` (the demand side), this file (the solution side).

## Rank 1 · EPF rejection decoder (ledger #1)

**Who solved it:** Australia (ATO lost-super consolidation via myGov, Protecting Your Super 2019); Netherlands (mijnpensioenoverzicht.nl statutory single pension view since 2011); UK Pensions Dashboards as the pitfall case.
**Mechanism:** Australia inverted the burden: funds must transfer inactive low-balance accounts to the tax office, which **proactively pushes the money into the member's active fund** with no application — ~4.7M accounts / A$7.1B auto-consolidated since Nov 2019, with the remaining A$16B lost-super figure published as a national statistic. The Netherlands gives one login showing all accrued pension across every employer, run by an independent foundation so no fund can hide a balance.
**Pitfalls there:** Australia's auto-sweep cancelled life/disability insurance attached to swept accounts (model what's attached before moving money). UK Pensions Dashboards: 23% cost overrun (£235M→£289M), one-year slip, NAO blames governance — the Dutch model is hard to retrofit. US SECURE 2.0 "Lost & Found" launched hollow because the IRS wouldn't share data: a registry without a mandatory data pipe is theatre.
**India, state-side (small):** publish the total unclaimed EPF corpus annually (the "₹X crore lying unclaimed" number creates its own pressure); rule that repeat rejections of the same claim escalate instead of looping.
**India, tool-side:** the defect-code decoder + cure path is exactly what these systems' status pages fail at — paste the remark → operative reason → cure → pre-filled joint declaration/grievance with the correct EPFiGMS category. No API needed.

## Rank 2 · GST SCN decoder (ledger #16)

**Who solved it:** Estonia (e-Business Register: rule-checked registration in ~30 min, no officer gate for the standard case); UK (HMRC VAT 40-working-day published standard, 98.3% within standard Oct 2025); Italy (silenzio-assenso, Law 241/1990 Art. 20 + the 2021 comma 2-bis upgrade); EU Services Directive Art. 13(4) makes tacit authorisation the default for service permits. Georgia: 30 days silence = licence deemed issued. (Lead check: NZ "instant GST" overstated — up to 10 working days; Singapore ACRA ~15 min but referral cases take weeks.)
**Mechanism:** three converging ideas: a closed list beats discretion (Estonia's structured form — an officer cannot free-text a demand); the clock is a published fact, not a negotiation (UK); and Italy's crucial 2021 fix: the administration must issue, on request, an **electronic attestation that deemed approval occurred** — and if it doesn't within 10 days, the citizen's own self-declaration legally substitutes. Silence leaves a receipt.
**Pitfalls there:** the UK's standard collapsed under the 2021-22 backlog + fraud attack (a published clock doesn't prevent backlog, it makes it contestable); deemed approval shifts risk onto the applicant (a tacitly approved non-compliant application can be annulled later); Italy's pre-2021 problem was exactly that the silence-approved citizen held no paper.
**India, state-side (small):** India already has the 7-working-day deemed-approval rule on paper. The missing halves are Italian: machine-issued deemed-approval attestation when the clock lapses, and a dropdown-only closed vocabulary of rejection grounds.
**India, tool-side:** the SCN decoder + clock tracker: map each demanded document against the legal REG-01 list, flag extra-legal demands with the Instruction 03/2025 citation, compute the reply deadline AND the deemed-approval date, draft a reply that answers only what the law requires and asserts the timeline.

## Rank 3 · Insurance ombudsman kit (ledger #12)

**Who solved it:** UK Financial Ombudsman Service (free, binding to £445k, ~33-36% overall uphold rate); Australia AFCA + its public "Datacube"; US ACA external review (45 CFR 147.136: independent review in 45 days / 72 hours expedited, binding).
**Mechanism:** the FOS/AFCA weapon is **published per-firm league tables**: any firm above a small threshold gets its complaint volume and uphold rate published by name (FOS half-yearly; AFCA's Datacube monthly). US external review: ~half of appealed denials are overturned (Health Affairs 2025; California DMHC reports 60-80% reversal).
**Pitfalls there:** the defining number: **fewer than 1% of denied US ACA claims are ever appealed** despite ~50% overturn — a binding remedy that nobody invokes is the norm, not the exception (identical to India's unknown ombudsman). FOS has backlog problems; reputational league tables carry no automatic penalty; SKGZ's small €37 fee still deters.
**India, state-side (small):** publish per-insurer complaint counts, ombudsman uphold rates, and "% of awards implemented within 30 days" as a half-yearly downloadable table. IRDAI holds the data; this costs a spreadsheet — and directly attacks the awards-ignored problem our Reddit scan found.
**India, tool-side:** lead with the base rate ("complaints like yours succeed ~X% of the time; the forum is free and binding"), then the structured drafter: rejection reason → what the insurer must have evidenced → pre-filled GRO/Bima Bharosa/Ombudsman complaint with statutory clocks. The <1% appeal rate is the market-size proof.

## Rank 4 · CPGRAMS false-closure appeal kit (ledger #5)

**Who solved it:** South Korea e-People/Sinmungo under the ACRC (~1.7M petitions/yr, ~300 bodies); NYC 311 (open data + published SLAs + published post-closure satisfaction); UK PHSO two-stage ombudsman.
**Mechanism:** Korea's three legs: statutory per-type clocks (actual averages ~6-7 days), post-answer satisfaction surveys published as a system metric (66.6%→84.4% over 4 years), and an independent second channel (ACRC ombudsman) so escalation doesn't return to the closer. NYC: every service request is a public open-data row, the SLA per complaint type is itself a published dataset, and closure triggers a published satisfaction survey — so third parties (state comptroller, journalists) audit closures. PHSO's design point: the appeal goes to a body institutionally outside the one that closed the complaint.
**Pitfalls there:** transparency enables auditing but doesn't enforce — the NYPD was credibly accused of falsifying 311 responses despite the open data; PHSO's slow second stage re-creates closure-by-exhaustion; a clock guarantees an answer, not a resolution.
**India, state-side (small):** publish CPGRAMS per-category disposal SLAs and per-ministry citizen-rating results monthly (NYC does both with zero new process); make closure text structured ("action taken" vs "why no action").
**India, tool-side:** the closure auditor: paste the closure reply → classify it (action / explanation / non-answer) → show the rate-Poor-then-appeal path with the 30-day clock → generate the appeal quoting the original grievance and closure verbatim. Keep the citizen's own timestamped record (filed date, SLA, closure date) — the audit trail the portal doesn't surface.

## Rank 5 · Aadhaar update rejection decoder (ledger #3)

**Who solved it:** US E-Verify's Tentative Nonconfirmation (mismatch) process — the single best analogue; EU GDPR Art. 16 rectification right + Art. 12(3) one-month clock; Estonia population-register self-check + once-only principle. (SSA record correction verified only partially: its public POMS evidence manual is the transplantable artifact, not its appeals.)
**Mechanism:** E-Verify never silently rejects: a mismatch produces a **Further Action Notice naming which agency's record failed and why**, a defined contest window (8 federal working days), and a named resolver — reason + deadline + named fixer, everything the one-word "Rejected" lacks. GDPR adds the legal clock and the duty to explain refusal and name the next forum. Estonia: corrected once, propagates everywhere (once-only law).
**Pitfalls there:** a right with a clock but a jammed enforcer still stalls (Ireland's DPC left 98% of significant cross-border cases unresolved); E-Verify puts the contest burden on the individual within a tight window; errors fall disproportionately on people with name changes.
**India, state-side (small):** every rejection must carry a reason code from a published closed list + the exact failed field + the named resolution path; no re-submission fee when the failure was an unpublished validator rule; publish the validator rulebook (the SSA-POMS move — the internal evidence manual is public and costs nothing to publish).
**India, tool-side:** pre-submission validator (check intended update against UIDAI's published document list and known failure patterns, estimate rejection risk BEFORE the fee) + rejection decoder + escalation drafts (grievance → CPGRAMS → RTI) with the GDPR framing: every refusal should state your next forum.

## Rank 6 · ITR refund hold triage (ledger #9)

**Who solved it:** US IRC §6611 (interest accrues automatically if refund not paid in 45 days) + GAO's public accounting of it; US Taxpayer Advocate Service (independent, named case advocates, Form 911); "Where's My Refund" as the anti-model; UK HMRC as the fairness pitfall.
**Mechanism:** §6611 makes delay cost the state money it must account for — $3.3B refund interest paid in FY2021 alone, ~$14B over seven years, with GAO criticizing the IRS for not tracking causes. TAS: economic-hardship cases get a named advocate empowered to issue orders to the division that caused the problem, plus an annual public "Most Serious Problems" report.
**Pitfalls there:** WMR's statuses are too vague to answer "why" — traffic rose 82% precisely because the tool said nothing (a status page without the operative reason reproduces the Indian failure); TAS is overwhelmed (22% of advocate orders late/unfulfilled — an under-scaled ombudsman is a second queue); HMRC pays 2.75% on delays while charging 7.75% to late payers — token interest doesn't discipline the state.
**India, state-side (small):** India already has s.244A interest; the missing piece is the GAO move — publish annually how much interest CBDT paid because of delayed refunds, broken down by cause. Plus a narrow TAS rule: third grievance on the same refund = named-officer escalation.
**India, tool-side:** Form-911-style triage, client-side: which lever fits (s.154 rectification vs s.245 offset response — the silent-consent trap with its 30-day window — vs e-Nivaran vs AO letter), draft it, and compute the s.244A interest already owed and state it in the draft. Never just restate portal status; always pair status with likely reason + the one action that moves it.

## Rank 7 · NSP scholarship pipeline decoder (ledger #7)

**Who solved it:** UK Student Finance England (published 6-8 week clock + crisp pay-on-registration trigger); US FAFSA 2024 as the pitfall case (GAO post-mortem); Germany BAföG as the parallel failure.
**Mechanism:** SFE publishes the clock and the trigger: apply on time → funded by term start; university confirms registration → payment in 3-5 working days. The student always knows which of three states they're in.
**Pitfalls there:** FAFSA shipped with 18 of 25 key requirements unmet, 40+ defects, a 25% call-centre answer rate, ~400,000 fewer (disproportionately low-income) applicants — helplines collapse exactly when defects spike. SFE's clock only protects on-time applicants (45% now miss the deadline). BAföG digitized the front door and kept the paper back office — NSP's exact architecture — and its portal went offline 3 weeks for a migration.
**India, state-side (small):** publish a per-stage SLA on the NSP status page (institute verification: X days; state: Y days) and a crisp disbursement trigger, so students can see WHICH stage is sitting on the file and for how long past its clock. Display-and-commitment change, near-zero build.
**India, tool-side:** stage-clock legibility without an API: student enters stage + dates → decode the defect code (UPLOAD INC etc.) with its correction deadline → compute days-sat vs guideline → draft the escalation to the RIGHT owner for that stage (institute nodal vs state/district nodal vs ministry — students routinely escalate to the wrong one). Same decoder architecture as EPF; only the dictionary differs.

## Rank 8 · Train seat evidence playbook (ledger #2)

**Who solved it:** EU Regulation 2021/782 (25%/60min, 50%/120min delay compensation + a common claim form since July 2024 that railways cannot refuse); UK Delay Repay (fixed bands, 28-day claim, 20-working-day response; operator c2c pays automatically, no claim filed); Japan JR (2+ hours late = the express/reserved surcharge refunded in full — the seat premium is separately priced and separately refundable).
**Mechanism:** a standard form + fixed percentage bands converts compensation from litigation into clerical processing; c2c proves auto-payout is operationally possible where the operator already knows journey + delay.
**Pitfalls there:** rights that require claims go unclaimed everywhere (ORR: most eligible UK passengers never claim — "too much hassle"); the EU recast added an extraordinary-circumstances exemption; the claims-company ecosystem (AirHelp: 35%, up to 50% with legal action) skims statutory money — the citizen tool should be the anti-AirHelp. Honest gap: no system directly compensates "someone sat in my reserved seat"; abroad it's solved upstream (conductor enforcement + refundable seat supplement), not downstream via damages.
**India, state-side (small):** a standard compensation claim form with fixed bands that Railways cannot reject for format; or the Japanese slice — treat the reservation charge as separately refundable, auto-refunded when the berth wasn't honoured (the TTE's berth record is the trigger).
**India, tool-side:** the evidence-discipline assistant for the existing consumer-court path: at the moment of occupation, prompt exactly what a claim needs (PNR, timestamped photo, TTE name, RailMadad entry), store it, later generate the filled complaint — AirHelp's real product is evidence-and-persistence packaging; give it away free.

## Rank 9 · Passport PV stall triage (ledger #4)

**Who solved it:** UK HMPO published processing standard (3 weeks; 99.7% within it in 2025) with its 2022 collapse as the pitfall; US congressional casework (privacy-release form + dedicated caseworkers + agency liaison desks obliged to respond); Canada (published standard + published actual: 78% in 2022 → 99% by May 2025).
**Mechanism:** the published norm converts a private stall into a measurable, attributable, escalatable public metric; the US pattern staffs a formal representative-escalation channel with forms and rules of engagement.
**Pitfalls there:** the standard fails exactly when needed most (UK 2022: 360k people past 10 weeks; the phone contractor answered 14% of calls, pushing everyone to MPs — 11,400 MP letters); casework is triage, advantages those who know the channel, and quietly substitutes for fixing the agency.
**India, state-side (small):** publish a per-district PV service standard with actuals ("90% of PV completes within X days in your district; current actual Y") — the data already sits in the PSP portal; it's a reporting change. Second: a standard MP-reference form + MEA liaison desk with an SLA.
**India, tool-side:** the stall detector: application date + district + stage → compare against crowdsourced/published typical timelines ("pending 47 days; district typical is 12") — manufacturing the norm-to-compare-against that the UK standard provides institutionally — then the written-channel escalation ladder (PSK grievance, RPO email, CPGRAMS, ready-to-sign MP letter). Everything written and reference-numbered, because the phone line is the first thing that dies.

## Rank 10 · Municipal false-closure fighter (ledger #6)

**Who solved it:** UK FixMyStreet (public-by-default reports since 2007, 1.1M+ reports); Chicago 311 (the same fake-closure failure + an ordinance response); San Diego (resident "before" photo paired with crew "after" photo); SeeClickFix (public comment threads + reopen rights). (Lead check: Boston "photo-verified closure" did not verify as a formal gate.)
**Mechanism:** FixMyStreet makes closure publicly falsifiable — every report, photo, response, and status sits on a public map, so "marked fixed" can be checked by anyone against visible reality. It needed no government cooperation: reports go by plain email; ~98% of councils simply accept them.
**Pitfalls there:** most FMS reports never show as "fixed" because councils don't send status back; reporting skews away from deprived areas (the public map can misallocate attention); SeeClickFix's open reopen right got gamed by trolls (lesson: reopen rights belong to the reporter, not everyone); Chicago's ordinance still depends on the same departments entering honest data.
**India, state-side (small):** display the crew's closure photo (geotagged, timestamped) publicly NEXT TO the citizen's original photo (San Diego pairing — a UI change); a reopen-once right for the original reporter with per-ward reopen rates published.
**India, tool-side:** the evidence mirror: help the citizen capture a timestamped/geotagged before photo, store complaint ID + closure evidence, and generate the side-by-side "closure vs reality" comparison for the reopen, the appeal, or public escalation. Frame outputs as evidence bundles, not adjudication.

## Rank 11 · Pension stall decoder (ledger #8)

**Who solved it:** UK "Tell Us Once" (register a death once, DWP fans notification to HMRC, DVLA, Passport Office, council, public pension schemes); Germany's Sterbevierteljahr (three months of the deceased's pension paid as an immediate income-test-free lump sum via Deutsche Post, often through the undertaker's paperwork; full scrutiny proceeds separately and offsets later); Sweden (survivor pension granted automatically from the population register, no application). US SSA as the pitfall (survivor benefits cannot be applied for online; staff cuts → months-long widow waits).
**Mechanism:** Germany pays first and verifies later — cash bridges the gap while bureaucracy grinds. Tell Us Once issues one reference number every agency accepts, so each office pulls instead of re-demanding.
**Pitfalls there:** Tell Us Once covers only government (banks/insurers still one-by-one) and families leave believing everything is handled; Sweden's automation depends on a reliable death register India's CRS only partially provides; a proactive design dies if the intake channel is the bottleneck (SSA).
**India, state-side (small):** make provisional family pension the DEFAULT with a clock (pay 3-6 months on death certificate + PPO while scrutiny continues, offset later — the CCS rules already permit provisional pension; the transplant is default-with-clock, not discretion). Plus the reference-number pattern from death registration.
**India, tool-side:** the fan-out checklist, inverted for a no-API world: from state + pension type + bank, generate the survivor's complete notification map in dependency order with each office's full document list assembled ONCE up front (pre-empting the piecemeal-demand loop), and surface "money you can get NOW" (provisional pension, gratuity, insurance) separately from "money after full processing."

## Rank 12 · UDID clock + appeals (ledger #14)

**Who solved it:** UK PIP (mandatory reconsideration → free independent tribunal, with quarterly published success rates: tribunals find for the claimant ~64-68%, and 91% of successful appeals present NO new evidence); Australia NDIS (60-day target, hard 90-day cap, and if the agency blows the clock the tribunal can take the case without waiting); US SSA (per-office wait times published; the waits themselves the pitfall).
**Mechanism:** the published success rate is itself the awareness mechanism — it teaches claimants that appealing works. NDIS adds a deadline with a deemed-escalation consequence rather than an aspiration.
**Pitfalls there:** PIP's internal reconsideration stage functions as an attrition filter — ~65% give up after the ~20-25%-success internal stage and never reach the two-thirds-success tribunal (a low-success internal stage placed before a high-success independent stage suppresses valid claims); SSA shows publishing wait times doesn't shorten them; the NDIS escape hatch only helps those who know it exists.
**India, state-side (small):** publish UDID processing times per district and appeal outcomes (sunlight on the 6-16x office variation); adopt the deemed-escalation rule — no medical-board decision in N days auto-escalates to the appellate authority (a rule amendment, not software).
**India, tool-side:** the single most tool-shaped finding of the whole research: tell users "91% of successful appeals used no new evidence — the appeal itself is the intervention." State the timeline, define "overdue" for their district, name the S.82 RPwD appeal authority with a pre-drafted representation, and show base rates to counter the learned helplessness that makes people quit.

## Rank 13 · Footpath obstruction router (ledger #11)

**Who solved it:** UK FixMyStreet's category→authority routing engine — the no-API mechanism in detail: (1) the map pin runs through MapIt (a boundary service) returning every administrative area containing that point; (2) a hand-maintained table maps report category → responsible authority type at that location; (3) delivery by plain email to the authority's published complaints address. No API, no MoU, no permission — councils started receiving formatted reports in 2007 whether they liked it or not. Seattle as the contrast/warning: crystal-clear owner-liability on paper (adjacent property owners own ~80-85% of sidewalks), zero enforcement ever, $500M+ backlog.
**Pitfalls there:** correct routing doesn't make anyone fix anything (most FMS reports never show fixed); boundary data and agency emails churn; India's overlapping authorities are messier than UK two-tier councils; naming an owner is worthless without enforcement/funding attached (Seattle).
**India, state-side (small):** publish per-ward a machine-readable "who owns what" table: category → responsible body → complaint email/portal. A spreadsheet, not a platform — the exact data layer FixMyStreet had to reverse-engineer.
**India, tool-side (the strongest architectural fit in the research):** replicate the three-step router client-side for ONE city: public ward GeoJSON polygons + a hand-curated category→agency table (corporation zonal office, PWD division, traffic police, smart-city SPV) → pre-addressed complaint draft or deep-link into the correct portal. The curation of the routing table IS the product. Log whether anything came back (FMS's missing loop).

## Rank 14 · Pothole injury compensation kit (ledger #10)

**Who solved it:** UK council highway claims (routine published claim forms — claiming is administrative, not litigation); NYC's prior-written-notice law + the Big Apple Pothole and Sidewalk Protection Committee (from 1982, trial lawyers surveyed and mapped defects citywide and FILED the maps with the DOT annually, mass-producing the "prior written notice" the law required — the best precedent anywhere for a citizen-side tool satisfying a legal precondition without government cooperation); New Zealand ACC (universal no-fault injury compensation) as the systemic alternative.
**Pitfalls there:** the UK channel is a rejection machine — 76-93% of claims rejected via the Section 58 "we had a reasonable inspection system" defence; one council spent £2,139 fighting a £190 claim. NYC killed the Big Apple loop legislatively and courts later let minor map-vs-reality discrepancies defeat notice (precision is the counter-weapon). NZ's no-fault still declines ~37% of treatment-injury claims.
**India, state-side (small):** a standard claim form per municipal body with a named officer and a decision deadline, plus annual publication of claims received/paid/rejected — even that legibility doesn't exist today.
**India, tool-side:** the Big Apple move adapted: (1) tell the user a compensation route exists in their city and what it requires; (2) the incident-moment evidence checklist — photo with exact location/timestamp, medical record, FIR, and PRIOR complaint history about that pothole (the analogue of prior notice, which also defeats the "we didn't know" defence); (3) draft the claim. Design against the NYC pitfall: capture location and defect description precisely so minor discrepancies can't sink the claim.

## Rank 15 · PM-JAY desk-denial rights card (ledger #15)

**Who solved it:** Thailand UCS (extra billing explicitly prohibited; single national number 1330 for questions AND complaints; published complaint ladder backed by a statutory investigation committee — complaint data shows it demonstrably captures desk denials); US No Surprises Act (patients legally owe in-network cost-sharing only; the payment fight moves to plan-vs-provider arbitration, removing the patient from the crossfire); US EMTALA as the enforcement-gap warning.
**Pitfalls there:** EMTALA: of 6,035 investigations (2002-15), 40% found violations but only 3.2% ended in monetary settlement — a right without prosecuted consequences decays into paperwork. Thailand still sees deposit-before-surgery cases; vulnerable groups don't know how to file. Philippines' "No Balance Billing" failed exactly like PM-JAY: hospitals eat delayed reimbursements and push costs to patients informally.
**India, state-side (small):** the Thai pattern: one memorable number + a PUBLISHED "what happens when you call" ladder (complaint → case number → investigation → refund/penalty) + a mandatory at-the-counter notice that refusing a valid card is a reportable empanelment violation. 14555 exists; the published case-numbered ladder and outcome statistics don't. Policy north star from the NSA: the reimbursement fight should be hospital-vs-state, never hospital-vs-patient at the desk.
**India, tool-side:** the desk-denial script + evidence kit: what to say, what to record (hospital, date, named person, informal charges — "asked to buy X outside" is evidence too, per the Philippine lesson), the 14555/SHA/DGRC route, a pre-drafted complaint citing the empanelment clause — with EMTALA-honest expectations: filing creates a record and pressure, not an instant remedy.

## Rank 16 · ESIC entitlements decoder (ledger #13)

**Who solved it:** UK NHS FP57 (a standardized at-the-counter refund receipt: ask at the moment of payment, claim at any pharmacy within 3 months); Germany §13(3) SGB V (a codified reimbursement right on "system failure" — if the fund can't provide an urgent benefit in time, the insured procures it and must be reimbursed — the exact legal shape of ESIC's out-of-stock rule, but litigable law instead of an obscure circular); Brazil SUS + free public defenders as the court-as-front-door warning.
**Pitfalls there:** FP57's fatal flaw is the transplant lesson: not asking at the counter means the right effectively evaporates — awareness-at-the-moment is everything. Germany's independent patient-advice service (UPD) collapsed when funded by the very sickness funds it advised against (payer-funded "independent" advice is structurally fragile — an argument FOR independent citizen-side tools). Brazil: litigation consumed 25% of the specialized pharma budget — court-as-front-door is a failure mode.
**India, state-side (small):** an ESIC FP57-equivalent: a standardized one-page refund-claim slip the pharmacy must hand over whenever a medicine is out of stock, stating the right, the documents, the deadline. A printed form, not an IT project.
**India, tool-side:** the moment-of-payment trigger: "Buying outside because the ESI pharmacy didn't have it? Photograph the prescription + bill NOW; here's the rule verbatim, the claim letter, where to submit." Frame it as a right on system failure, not a favor; administrative remedy first, never lead with litigation.

## Rank 17 · FSSAI tier + renewal clock (ledger #17)

**Who solved it:** UK food business registration (free, one form, 28 days before opening, CANNOT be refused, and NO renewal, ever — a notification, not an application; risk handled downstream by risk-scored inspections + public 0-5 hygiene ratings); NSW Food Notify (free online notification with an instant confirmation certificate); US county health permits as the opposite pole.
**Mechanism insight:** the UK deliberately unbundled registration from risk control. There is no tier for the business to guess and no renewal cliff to fall off; everyone registers once, inspection frequency carries the risk logic.
**Pitfalls there:** notification-only relocates the state's work to inspection capacity — and the UK's is currently failing (~95,000 overdue inspections, 871 high-risk businesses unvisited, staff down 9.1% while businesses grew 5.7%). Free-and-unrefusable doesn't remove work; it moves it.
**India, state-side (small):** kill the renewal cliff for the lowest tier (perpetual registration + duty-to-update); short of that, auto-renewal by default with consent-based fee auto-debit and a statutory 90/30/7-day notice sequence before any late fee accrues.
**India, tool-side:** tier calculator + renewal deadman switch: questionnaire → Registration vs State vs Central with the rule citation shown; store expiry locally, calendar reminders at 90/60/30 days, the lapse-rescue path, and the honest "expiry+180 days = licence dies" warning. (Note: our complaint scan ranked this low on demand; if built, it's a module, not a product.)

## Rank 18 · Municipal trade licences (ledger #18)

**Who solved it:** Georgia's 2005 regulatory guillotine (84% of licence requirements abolished; survivors carry a 30-day clock after which the licence is deemed issued — converting inspector foot-dragging from leverage into self-defeat); Canada BizPaL (a cross-government tool that tells any business exactly which permits it needs across federal/provincial/municipal — 1,100+ municipalities; it grants nothing, it makes the requirement-space legible); Kenya eCitizen (single portal, payment through the platform not the inspector's hand).
**Pitfalls there:** Georgia's deregulation overshot — food-safety inspection was suspended for years and had to be rebuilt for the EU trade agreement (a guillotine is a one-shot political act, not a maintenance regime); BizPaL is information-only and depends on 1,100 jurisdictions maintaining their own entries; Kenya's platform became its own toll booth (the KSh 50 convenience fee ruled unlawful; private intermediaries earned KSh 1.45B in a year — digitizing the counter can just move the rent).
**India, state-side (small):** deemed issuance for RENEWALS specifically (re-vetting an existing licensee is the lowest-risk place for a 30-day clock); a BizPaL-style machine-readable licence register per ULB (a spreadsheet mandate).
**India, tool-side:** a BizPaL-pattern "which licences does my shop need here" mapper over a hand-curated dataset for the top cities, each answer linking the municipal rule PDF and the official fee so any demand above it is instantly visible. (Complaint scan found no standalone demand; folds into a future compliance umbrella.)

## The five patterns that repeat across all 18

1. **Closed lists beat discretion.** Estonia's structured forms, E-Verify's reason codes, dropdown-only rejection grounds. The officer who cannot free-text a demand cannot invent one.
2. **The clock is the right, and silence must leave a receipt.** Deemed approval (Italy, Georgia, EU) works only with the 2021 Italian addition: an attestation the citizen can hold when the clock lapses. India repeatedly has the clock on paper and lacks the receipt.
3. **Published norms convert private stalls into public metrics.** UK/Canada passport standards, NYC SLAs, FOS/AFCA league tables, HMCTS tribunal success rates, GAO's refund-interest accounting. Publishing is a spreadsheet-cost act with outsized behavioral effect — and the honest caveat is that standards break under surges and sunlight alone doesn't enforce.
4. **Rights that require claims go unclaimed everywhere.** <1% of denied US health claims appealed (despite ~50% overturn), most UK Delay Repay unclaimed, UK pothole claims a rejection machine, PIP's internal stage shedding 65% of eventual winners. This is the exact global gap our citizen-side tools occupy — and the fee-skimming ecosystem (AirHelp's 35-50%) shows the wrong way to fill it.
5. **Every mechanism has a documented counterattack.** NYPD falsifying open-data responses, Section 58 paperwork defences, precision challenges to the Big Apple maps, payer-funded advice services collapsing (Germany's UPD), digitized counters becoming toll booths (Kenya). Treat these as the threat model for anything we build: assume the office adapts, and design the evidence trail for that.

**The single most reusable line for the case study** (from the UK PIP data): *91% of successful appeals presented no new evidence — the appeal itself is the intervention.* That is the thesis of the whole board, measured.
